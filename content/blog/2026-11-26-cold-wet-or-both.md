**Cold, wet or both**

There’s a picnic table in a tiny park down at the end of the street. Underneath the full moon with Venus, looking out toward the east, it’s all dark, except for these generous gifts of light from the sky. But even with that, it’s still dark. It’s the seat on this picnic table, where I often sit and meditate with my dog at my feet. The thing is, when it’s really cold, it’s hard to tell if the seat plank is cold, or wet with dew / irrigation spray or both cold and wet.



I’m sure I could look up how the brain might be confused by cold and wet. But I don’t need a diagnosis. I just want to make a point. I can’t easily include which of these it is. If it’s wet, I won’t sit down there for 10 minutes to meditate. If it’s dry and cold, I will. I don’t mind calling so much. I do mind wet. As soon as I stand up insert walking, the cold goes away. But, the wet will stay with me for a while.



We often make predictive mistakes. One thing looks or feels like another to us. But to complicate things even more, both things can be true at once.



You’re not a bad person if you make a mistake telling if it’s cold or wet or both. Multiple things can be true at once. And, it’s a function of our brain's desire for certainty to say it has to be one or the other, not both.



This lean towards certainty isn’t always protective. For me, at the bench seat, if I make a mistake, it can color my day. My day seems to go better, I’d like to think that I’m less irritable, that I look forward to the day once I’ve had that brief sit. So if I jump to the conclusion that it’s wet instead of cold, it’s imposing a kind of dark cloud over the rest of the day. You see what I mean? Am I explaining this well to you as my Reader / Therapist? 



Today is Thanksgiving here in the United States, Texas in particular. Since it’s easy to make mistakes, some of the pilgrims who landed off the shores of the United States stayed in their boats for long periods of time. They didn’t know the strangers who were on the land. They didn’t trust them. As for the American Indians, who saw them in the distance offshore, they knew they must be suffering. Eventually, help and need, suspicion and compassion, overcame the barriers that humans so often place on our relationships to one another. We have been taught to fear by millennia of evolution. We would benefit by asking ourselves. What other possibilities are there? What else can we learn? Will our lives be saved by venturing forth towards cooperation?
