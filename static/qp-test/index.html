Grief is one of the most human things there is, and it rarely follows a map. This is a quiet place to put down some of what you are carrying: your thoughts, your memories, the heavy days and the lighter ones.

Grief is unique. Grief is work. Grief is a process. There is no right way to do it, and nothing here that you have to finish. Come when you want. Leave when you need to. You are welcome here exactly as you are, whatever you believe and however you are grieving.

You may be grieving more than one person, or more than one loss. You can keep a page for each person you miss, and say who each entry is about. Nothing has to be sorted out now.

Your words stay with you

Everything you write here is kept on this computer, in this browser. Nothing is sent anywhere. There is no account and no one else can read it. If you ever choose to share something, that is always your decision, and nothing happens unless you ask for it.

Please use the same browser, on the same device, each time you come back. A different browser or computer will show an empty journal, and so will clearing your browser's data. Your safety net is a backup: a copy of your writing, saved as a small file.

The Four Questions

After a death, much of the world has to be learned again: who you are, who they are to you now, how you stand with the people you shared, and how you live in the wider world. The philosopher Thomas Attig, in How We Grieve: Relearning the World, describes grieving in just this way. These four questions mark the main places where that relearning happens.

They are not steps or stages, and you do not have to answer them in order or finish any of them. They come around again and again, like a spiral staircase that brings you back to the same view from a different height.

The answers will not come all at once. That's okay. Some may take months or years. What helps is often not the answer but the asking: writing a few honest lines, coming back later, and noticing how your answer changes.

How to use this page: choose who you are thinking of (or type a name), read a question and the prompts under it, write whatever comes (one sentence is enough), and save. Your earlier writing stays under each question, so you can see how your thinking changes.

Learning About Grief

Please don't ignore your body, especially your heart

Deep grief can have big, and sometimes serious, effects on how the heart works. Bereavement carries real cardiovascular risks, and they should not be ignored. In the first days and weeks after a loss, the risk of a heart attack is higher than usual, and a stress-related heart condition sometimes called "broken heart syndrome" is real.

Whenever you have chest pain, call emergency medical services (911 in the U.S.). Don't wait to see if it passes. Tell your doctor that you are grieving, and keep up with your checkups.

Grief is unique, grief is work, and grief is a process. Many people wonder, quietly, whether what they're going through is normal. Here are some of the most common experiences. Open whichever ones speak to you.

Grief lives in the body

Grief is not only emotional. Your whole body mourns along with you. Common body reactions include:

  • Chest pain or pressure, or trouble breathing: call emergency medical services. Don't wait. Bereavement has distinct cardiovascular risks.
  • Exhaustion, trouble sleeping, or sleeping too much.
  • Changes in appetite, an upset stomach, or nausea.
  • Headaches, backaches, muscle aches, or a tight jaw and shoulders.
  • A lump in the throat, sighing, or a heavy feeling in the chest.
  • Dizziness, weakness, or feeling shaky; sometimes even rashes.
  • Catching every cold going around, because stress and poor sleep affect the immune system.
  • Restlessness, a surprising rush of energy, or tears that come without warning.

Not every symptom is "just grief." When something is new, severe, or worrying, please ask a doctor. In the meantime, be gentle with yourself: eat and drink regularly, move a little, let others help, and set limits you can keep. You do not have to do all of it at once.

There is no timeline

Grief does not follow a schedule. Some people feel steadier in a few months; many find that the second year is harder than the first, because the world has moved on while they are still missing someone. If you are still hurting long after others expect you to be "over it," that is not a failing. Love does not have an expiration date.

Grief is a spiral, not a straight line

Grief is a verb, not a noun. It is active, fluid, and involves your whole being. Many people picture it as a spiral staircase: some days you move up a few stairs, and other days you slip down a few. You may pass the same landing more than once, and that is not going backward. It is how the work is done.

It also comes in waves. A song, a smell, or a grocery aisle can lift a wave out of nowhere. Waves tend to come further apart and soften over time, but they can still surprise you years later.

The fog: forgetting, distraction, feeling "off"

Many grieving people lose their keys, forget appointments, read the same paragraph four times, or feel as though they are moving through fog. Your mind is doing enormous work in the background. It usually lifts gradually. In the meantime, write things down and lower your expectations of yourself.

Numbness, or feeling nothing at all

Numbness is one of the mind's ways of protecting you when a loss is too big to take in all at once. It can show up at the funeral, weeks later, or in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday. It does not mean you didn't love them. Feeling often returns on its own schedule.

Anger, guilt, and the "if only"s

Anger at the person who died, at doctors, at family, or at yourself is very common, and so is guilt. "If only I had called more, noticed sooner, said that one thing." These thoughts are part of the mind trying to find some control in something that could not be controlled.

Almost always, the reason we made the choices we did was love. We do the best we can with the information we have and the pressure of the moment. It can help to remember the circumstances more accurately, to learn what there is to learn, and to bless and forgive ourselves. Saying these thoughts aloud or writing them down can take some of the weight out of them.

Unfinished business and things left unsaid

After a death, many people regret things they said, or didn't say. Some write a letter to the person as if they could read it. Some speak aloud to an empty chair, saying everything they still want to say. Both are well-worn paths for working through unresolved feelings, and you can do either one here, in the Write page.

It also helps to remember the person as they really were: fully human, both the beautiful and the difficult. That is how we honor, and grieve, the real person.

Relief mixed with sadness

After a long illness or a hard relationship, some people feel relief alongside the grief and then feel ashamed of the relief. Relief and sorrow can sit side by side. Both can be true, and neither cancels the love.

Grieving before the death (anticipatory grief)

When someone is dying, or slowly changing through illness, many people begin to grieve long before the death. This can go on for days, weeks, months, or even years. With a slow decline such as dementia, you may say goodbye in small pieces: a name that is forgotten, a role that changes, a conversation that no longer comes.

It is real grief. It often brings tiredness, tears, guilt, and mixed feelings, including wishing it were over and then feeling ashamed of wishing. You are not wrong or disloyal for feeling any of it, and caregivers in particular carry a heavy load.

Grieving ahead of time does not necessarily make the grief afterward smaller. For some people it is softer, and for others it still arrives with full force. Both are ordinary. If you are caring for someone now, the Write page is a good place to put some of this down, and it is fine to ask others for help.

When a child dies

The death of a child turns the natural order upside down. Many parents say it is the hardest loss there is, whatever their child's age. (Losing a grown child is described in the next topic.)

  • There is no "getting over it." Many parents describe learning to carry the loss rather than leaving it behind.
  • Parents grieve differently, and at different speeds. Different is not the same as not caring. Strain between partners is common and understandable. Patience, honest talk, and sometimes a counselor can help.
  • Guilt is almost universal, even when nothing could have been done. Parents are meant to protect their children, and it can feel like failing at that. Often the guilt is love looking for something to do.
  • Other children are grieving too, in their own ways, and may be quietly trying to protect you. They need honest words ("died," not "lost" or "gone") and permission to ask questions.
  • Everyday moments can hurt: "How many children do you have?", other families' milestones, a school year that begins without them, birthdays, and the date of the death. It helps to plan ahead for the hard days.
  • Other people may say the wrong thing. You get to choose who you lean on, and you can tell them what actually helps.

The Compassionate Friends offers support groups for families after the death of a child, sibling, or grandchild, at any age and from any cause. Hospice and hospital bereavement programs can also point you to groups near you.

When an adult child dies

Parents of grown children are sometimes overlooked, as if the loss belonged mostly to the child's partner and children. But a parent has lost the child they raised, along with the future they pictured, and often a friend and helper in their own later years.

  • Your grief is real, and it is not smaller because your child was grown.
  • It can be hard to find room for it. Attention often goes to the child's partner and children, and parents may feel they must be strong for them, or for each other. Outliving a child can feel deeply wrong.
  • You may be worrying about grandchildren or a daughter- or son-in-law while barely holding yourself together. It's fine to ask for help too.
  • Practical losses can pile up: the person you called every Sunday, who drove you to appointments, or looked in on you. Naming those losses one at a time can make them easier to carry.
  • Family tensions can surface over the funeral, belongings, or your place in the grandchildren's lives. Patience, and a trusted person to talk to, help.
  • In later life, health and loneliness add weight. Keep up with your doctor, and reach out to people.

The Compassionate Friends welcomes parents of children of any age, and hospice and hospital bereavement programs often welcome the whole community.

Anniversaries, holidays, and "ordinary" hard days

Birthdays, anniversaries, holidays, and even a certain time of year can bring grief rushing back, sometimes before you realize why. It is a normal part of remembering. It helps to plan ahead: some people light a candle and share memories with family, visit a special place, write a letter, or create a simple ritual of their own. Others need to do something completely different that day. Either is fine. Decide ahead of time what you will keep, what you will change, and who you might lean on. On a keepsake page, you can add a yearly reminder to your own calendar.

People grieve differently

Within the same family, one person may cry openly, another may throw themselves into tasks, and another may go quiet. None of these is the "right" way, and they are easy to misread as not caring. Many people find it helps to say aloud, "I grieve differently than you do, and I still love you."

Losing more than one person

One person plays many roles in a lifetime. When a husband dies, so do a best friend, a confidant, a co-parent, a handyman, a gardener, a trusted advisor. It can take months to realize all that was lost, and grief can seem to strike anew with each realization. Naming the roles you miss, one at a time, can make the loss more understandable.

When the loss was sudden

An unexpected death changes a person's world in an instant, and there may have been no chance for goodbyes. Repeated "what ifs," vivid replays, disturbing dreams, anxiety, and a loss of the feeling of safety are all common after a sudden death. All grief deserves support, and sudden loss especially deserves it. Please consider reaching out for help from someone trained to walk with you through it.

Grief and depression: neighbors, not strangers

Picture a weight on a string, swinging freely. That is usual life: a wide swing of joy and sadness, laughter and worry. In grief, it is as if a magnet sits slightly below. The swing narrows and leans low, but it still swings. A song, a memory, or a grandchild's laugh can still push it up.

If the magnet gets very close, the swing nearly disappears. That is more like depression, where the low mood becomes constant, joy and interest fade, feelings of worthlessness or guilt grow, and everything feels gray. In grief, by contrast, the longing is for the person you lost, self-worth is usually intact, and humor and warmth can still break through.

Grief can slide toward depression when the mind keeps replaying the same loop, when we pull away from people, when sleep and eating suffer, when we have had depression before or many losses have piled up, or when we cared for someone for a long time before they died.

One helpful difference: brooding circles ("Why me? What if?" over and over), while reflecting moves ("What am I learning? What do I need?"). Writing here can help turn brooding into reflecting. And the magnet can ease back. Recovery is rarely a trumpet. It is usually a glimpse: a morning you tasted your coffee, a laugh that surprised you. If you want to keep an eye on the signs, the Check-In page has a list, and you can bring it to your doctor.

Meaning, faith, and questions without answers

Loss often stirs deep questions: Why did this happen? Where are they now? What is my life for, now? Some people find comfort in their faith, some find it shaken, some find new spiritual or philosophical ground, and some find that the questions simply stay open. All of these are normal parts of grieving. You do not need to settle the big questions to get through today. Often, the significance lies in giving yourself permission to ask.

Awe, beauty, and moments that feel like signs

Many bereaved people describe moments that feel like a presence, a vivid dream, a song at exactly the right time, a bird that lingers, or a coincidence too well-timed to ignore. These experiences are very common and, for most people, comforting. Some understand them as spiritual, some as the mind's way of staying connected, and some leave the question open. You do not have to decide what they mean, and they are not a sign that something is wrong with you.

Beauty can also reach us in grief: a sky, a piece of music, a stranger's kindness. Being moved by it does not mean you have stopped grieving. It can be one of the ways love keeps reaching you.

When faith is part of your grief

For many people, faith is a real comfort in loss. Prayer, sacred reading, ritual, music, and the company of a faith community can carry you when your own strength runs low.

For others, loss shakes their faith, and that can feel frightening or lonely. Anger, doubt, and silence toward God or the Divine are very old companions of grief, and many traditions make room for them. The Psalms, for example, are full of honest lament. You can bring your whole self, questions included, to whatever you hold sacred.

It is also fine if your faith comforts you one day and feels distant the next. A chaplain, pastor, priest, rabbi, imam, or other spiritual guide can sit with you without needing you to have it all figured out.

What tends to help

The single most effective thing for many people is to give expression to their feelings: talking, writing, praying, singing, painting, exercising, even a good cry. Other things many people find helpful, in small doses: telling the story of the loss to someone who will really listen; time outdoors; simple routines for sleep and meals; spending time with people who do not rush you; and naming a few things that still bring even a flicker of comfort. Be gentle with yourself. You do not have to do all of them.

Laughter belongs too. Tears of laughter can be as healing as tears of grief, and enjoying a calm moment is not a betrayal of the one you miss. It is part of how you keep going.

When grief needs more than time

Most grief, however painful, gradually softens with time and support. But if you find yourself unable to manage ordinary daily tasks, or you are withdrawing from others, or the pain stays at full intensity for many months, or you have thoughts of not wanting to live, it may be time to bring in a professional. That is a sign of strength, not weakness. See the page.

Reaching Out

If you are in crisis or thinking of ending your life

These thoughts are more common than people say, especially after a loss. They do not mean you are weak. They mean you are in pain and need support. You do not have to carry this alone.

In the United States, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) at any hour, day or night. You can also text HOME to 741741 (Crisis Text Line). If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Outside the U.S., please contact your local emergency number or crisis line.

Don't ignore body symptoms, especially your heart

Deep grief can have big, and sometimes serious, effects on how the heart works. Bereavement has distinct cardiovascular risks that should not be ignored.

Whenever you have chest pain, call emergency medical services (911 in the U.S.). Don't wait to see if it passes, and don't drive yourself.

Other warning signs include pressure or squeezing in the chest; pain spreading to the arm, shoulder, neck, jaw, or back; trouble breathing; a cold sweat; nausea; or feeling faint. In women and older adults the signs can be less obvious. When in doubt, call.

Signs it may be time to talk with someone

  • You can't take care of the basics: eating, sleeping, hygiene, work, or caring for others.
  • Your pain feels just as intense after many months, with no softening at all.
  • You are using alcohol, pills, or other substances to get through the day.
  • You feel you can't go on without them, or you wish you could join them.
  • You feel stuck in guilt, self-blame, or anger that will not ease.
  • You have lost interest in nearly everything for weeks at a time.
  • The people around you are worried about you.

Any one of these is a good reason to reach out. You do not have to be "bad enough." If you tell your doctor, mention your mood and sleep. Counseling, and sometimes medicine, help many people, and your Check-In dates can help them see the whole picture.

Where to find support

  • Hospice bereavement programs. Many hospices offer grief support to the whole community, often free, even if your loved one was not in their care. Call the one nearest you and ask.
  • Your doctor or a licensed counselor or therapist. Ask specifically for someone experienced with grief and loss.
  • Support groups. Hospitals, community centers, and funeral homes often host them. If your child has died, The Compassionate Friends offers groups for parents, siblings, and grandparents, at any age and from any cause.
  • Trusted people. Tell one person how you are really doing this week.
  • SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357, free and confidential, 24/7, for help finding mental health or substance use treatment.

Spiritual care

Hospital and hospice chaplains, clergy, spiritual directors, and members of your faith community can offer confidential listening, and prayer or ritual if you wish. You usually do not need to be a member of a congregation. Many are glad simply to sit with you.

A note about this journal: It is a companion for reflection and understanding. It is not therapy, medical care, or emergency help, and it can't replace a real person who knows you.

Save a backup

What is a backup? A backup is a copy of everything you have written, saved as a small file. Think of it as a photocopy of your journal, kept in a safe drawer.

Why make one? Your journal lives only in this browser, on this computer. If the browser's data is cleared, or the computer is replaced or breaks, the writing would be gone. A backup file lets you bring it back, or move it to another computer.

Step by step: saving and restoring a backup

To save a backup

  1. Press Save a backup.
  2. A small file with a name like grief_journal_backup_2026-11-22.json goes to your Downloads folder (or wherever your browser keeps downloads). Your browser may show a small message or arrow when it is done.
  3. Open your Downloads folder and move or copy that file somewhere safe: your Documents folder, a USB stick, or a cloud folder you already use. That is all there is to it.

A good habit is to save a backup once a month, or any time you have written something you would hate to lose. The journal will remind you when it has been a while. Each backup holds everything, so you only need to keep the newest one.

To restore a backup (for example, on a new computer, or if your journal looks empty)

  1. Open the journal in the browser you want to use.
  2. Press Restore from a backup.
  3. Choose your backup file. If the backup is locked with a password, you will be asked for it.

Restoring only adds what is missing. It never erases or overwrites what is already in the journal, so it is safe to try.

If you can't find your file, look in your Downloads folder for a name that starts with grief_journal_backup.

If you have turned on a password, your backup is locked with it too.
